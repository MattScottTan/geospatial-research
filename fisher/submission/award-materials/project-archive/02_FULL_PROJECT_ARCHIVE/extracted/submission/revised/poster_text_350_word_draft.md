## Draft narrative
Food similarity is spatial, but not simply because nearby places eat alike. This project asks where cuisine similarity follows geographic distance, where it exceeds distance-based expectation, and which places become bridge nodes in the residual geography of food.

I represent cuisines as filtered ingredient profiles from a recipe corpus, normalize ingredient names, map cuisine labels to approximate geographic anchors, and calculate pairwise cuisine similarity. A distance baseline estimates how similar two cuisines should be given their geographic separation. The key GIS result is the residual: observed similarity minus distance-predicted similarity. Positive residuals become candidate culinary corridors.

The global model is used as a discovery screen rather than a causal explanation. Stronger interpretation comes from focused cases. East/Southeast Asia is the primary case because the retained cuisines form a coherent regional and cross-subregional geography. A residual bridge-index analysis then aggregates pairwise residual links into mapped place-level roles, showing which cuisines participate most strongly in unexpected spatial connections.

The project does not claim that the recipe corpus represents all world cuisine, nor that migration, trade, colonialism, or maritime exchange caused any specific link. Those mechanisms remain hypotheses for future testing with explicit historical and flow data. The contribution is geospatial: GIS turns food similarity into a map-based method for finding corridors, bridges, and boundaries that ingredient clustering alone cannot reveal.
