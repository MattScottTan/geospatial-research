# Revised Abstract and Pitch

Created: 2026-04-29

## 150-250 word abstract
**Culinary Corridors** uses GIS to ask when cuisine similarity follows geographic distance and when it breaks distance-based expectation. I represent cuisines as filtered ingredient profiles, calculate pairwise similarity, assign cuisine labels to approximate geographic anchors, and fit a distance baseline. The residuals - observed similarity minus predicted similarity - become candidate culinary corridors: cuisine pairs whose ingredient similarity is stronger than geography alone would suggest. The project uses a global 20-cuisine screen as a discovery layer, then focuses interpretation on East/Southeast Asia, where the retained cuisines form the clearest regional/cross-subregional case. A residual bridge-index analysis then aggregates pairwise residuals into mapped place-level roles, showing which cuisines act as bridge nodes in the residual geography of food. The project does not claim that recipe-platform data represent all world cuisine, nor does it prove that migration, trade, colonialism, or maritime exchange caused the observed similarities. Instead, it demonstrates a Fisher-style GIS contribution: spatial modeling turns food similarity into evidence about corridors, bridges, and boundaries that cannot be found through ingredient clustering alone.

## 60-90 second pitch
Most food projects ask what cuisines have in common. My project asks where those commonalities become spatially surprising. I represent cuisines as ingredient profiles, measure how similar each cuisine pair is, and then compare that similarity to geographic distance. The key GIS move is the residual: if two cuisines are more similar than a distance-only model predicts, that pair becomes a candidate culinary corridor. The global model is only a discovery screen. The strongest interpretation comes from a focused East/Southeast Asia case, where residual links remain spatially organized after generic ingredients are filtered. I also compute residual bridge scores, which turn pairwise food links into mapped place-level roles. That means the project is not just mapping recipes; GIS produces the central evidence. It shows where food follows distance, where it breaks distance, and which cuisines become bridges in the residual geography of food. The claims are deliberately cautious: the results suggest spatial associations and candidate corridors, not causal proof of migration or trade.
