# Abstract and Pitch

Created: 2026-04-29

## 150–250 word abstract

**Culinary Corridors** treats cuisine as spatial evidence. Using a recipe-derived ingredient corpus, the project represents each cuisine as an ingredient profile, computes pairwise cuisine similarity, and compares that similarity against geographic distance. The central question is not simply which cuisines are similar, but which similarities are unexpected once geography is taken into account. A distance-only model creates a baseline; positive residuals become candidate “culinary corridors” that can be mapped and interpreted spatially. The global 20-cuisine model is used as a discovery screen rather than causal proof. Stronger interpretation comes from a focused East/Southeast Asia case, where residual similarities among Chinese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, Filipino, and Japanese cuisines form a coherent regional and cross-subregional pattern. The project also introduces residual bridge scores, which aggregate pairwise residuals into mapped place-level roles and reveal which cuisines act as spatial bridges in the residual network. The results suggest that cuisine similarity is spatially structured but not reducible to distance. By combining ingredient similarity, distance baselines, residual corridor mapping, and focused regional analysis, the project demonstrates how GIS can reveal hidden spatial structure in cultural and food data while maintaining careful limits around causality and representativeness.

## 60–90 second oral pitch

Food is usually discussed as culture, history, or taste. In this project, I ask what happens when we also treat it as spatial evidence. I represent cuisines as ingredient profiles, measure how similar they are, and then compare that similarity against geographic distance. The key move is the residual: if two cuisines are more similar than a distance-only model predicts, that pair becomes a candidate culinary corridor.

The global map is not meant to prove a causal story. It is a discovery screen that shows where unexpected similarities appear. The strongest interpretation comes from a focused East and Southeast Asia case, where cuisine similarities among Chinese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, Filipino, and Japanese labels form a coherent regional pattern after generic ingredient filtering.

The most GIS-specific part is the residual bridge score. Instead of only mapping lines between similar cuisines, I aggregate residual corridors into place-level bridge roles. That lets the map show which cuisines repeatedly connect across distance. The result is a project where GIS is not just a presentation tool; it produces the insight by showing where food follows geography, where it breaks geography, and where spatial corridors emerge.

## 2–3 sentence ultra-short description

Culinary Corridors maps cuisine similarity against geographic distance to identify where food is more similar than geography alone predicts. The project uses global screening, focused East/Southeast Asia analysis, and residual bridge scores to show that cuisine similarity is spatially structured but not reducible to distance. It is a GIS project because the key insight comes from spatial baselines, residual corridors, and mapped bridge roles—not from ingredients alone.
