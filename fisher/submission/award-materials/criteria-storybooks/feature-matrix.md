## Prize anchor

- **Canonical competition identity:** Treat the target competition as the **Fisher Prize for Excellence in GIS (Howard T. Fisher Prize in GIS)** administered through Harvard University's Center for Geographic Analysis (CGA).
- **Canonical official award page:** [S1] `https://gis.harvard.edu/awards`
- **Canonical official competition page for current judging language:** [S2] `https://gis.harvard.edu/event/fisher-prize-award-competition`
- **Identity normalization rule:** When official Harvard CGA sources alternate between "Howard T. Fisher Prize in GIS" and "Fisher Prize for Excellence in GIS," treat them as the same prize family unless a future official source explicitly says otherwise.
- **Working prize description from official snippets:** the prize was established in 1999, is awarded annually, and recent CGA materials describe one undergraduate and one graduate winner for excellence in GIS at Harvard.[S1][S2]
- **Scope note:** This analysis is limited to the Harvard CGA Fisher Prize materials matching the five published judging criteria below.

## Citation scheme

- **Source IDs:** use the source-inventory IDs already defined in this file. Official Fisher / Harvard sources use the `S#` prefix; recoverable primary project materials use the `P#` prefix.
- **In-text citation format:** place source IDs in square brackets at the end of the sentence or bullet they support, for example `[S5][P2]`.
- **Multiple-source rule:** when a claim depends on both the official winner record and the primary project material, cite both source types rather than only one.
- **Storybook carry-forward rule:** all six storybook files reuse the same source IDs defined in this matrix; they should not invent a second citation namespace.
- **Inference rule:** when a statement combines evidence rather than quoting a source directly, label it explicitly with `Inference:` and then cite the supporting source IDs.
- **Coverage-note rule:** when a claim depends on incomplete or indirect evidence, pair the citation with the appropriate uncertainty label from the next section instead of presenting the claim as fully settled.

## Conflict-resolution rule

- **Program-level conflicts:** when official Fisher/CGA sources disagree about prize naming, category labels, or judging-criteria wording, use the **most recent official Fisher competition page** as the governing source for normalization.[S2][S3]
- **Cycle-level conflicts:** for winner names, project titles, and cycle-specific details, prefer the official source tied to that specific cycle (for example a year-specific winner page, archive snippet, or Gazette announcement) over a generic archive page.[S4][S5][S6][S7][S10][S11][S12][S13][S14]
- **Access-limitation rule:** if a direct page fetch fails but the page is still indexed with a readable official snippet, treat the snippet as admissible official evidence and note the limitation instead of discarding the cycle.
- **Historical-preservation rule:** if an older official page preserves detail that newer archive pages omit, keep the older detail for that cycle while still using the newest official page to normalize the overall rubric and prize identity.

## Uncertainty labels

- **`covered`** — official winner evidence exists and the current public source set exposes enough primary project material to support a five-criterion scoring pass.
- **`partial`** — official winner evidence exists, but the primary project material is missing, inaccessible, truncated, or too thin for a defensible full scoring pass.
- **`unavailable`** — no public official winner evidence has been recovered for that cycle in the current search.
- **`uncertain-link`** — the primary material is a strong title/author/topic match to the winner, but the direct Fisher-page hyperlink cannot be verified because the official page is inaccessible or incomplete.
- **`uncertain-title`** — the official source exposes only a truncated or incomplete title.
- **`uncertain-inference`** — a later analytical claim is synthesized from multiple pieces of evidence rather than explicitly stated in a single source.

## Weighting logic

- **No official numeric weights are published in the current Fisher criteria pages.** For comparison purposes, this matrix therefore uses an **internal equal-weight benchmark** across the five official judging criteria.
- **Weight assignment:**
  - Innovation and creativity of chosen topic — **20%**
  - Use of GIS in performing the project — **20%**
  - Data: complexity, relevance, documentation — **20%**
  - Analytical approach and execution — **20%**
  - Visualization / cartographic communication effectiveness — **20%**
- **Overall comparison score:** for each `covered` winner, compute the simple weighted average of the five criterion scores. Because the weights are equal, this is equivalent to the mean of the five 1–5 scores.
- **Scoring gate:** only winner cases labeled `covered` receive a full numeric scoring pass. Cases labeled `partial` or `unavailable` remain unscored (`n/a`) so that the matrix does not convert missing evidence into false precision.
- **Uncertainty rule:** if a scored winner relies on `uncertain-link`, `uncertain-title`, or `uncertain-inference`, keep the numeric score but record the flag in the matrix so the reader can distinguish strong evidence from plausible reconstruction.
- **Interpretation rule:** the overall score is an internal benchmark for cross-winner comparison, not a claim about the judges’ hidden weighting or exact historical scorecards.

## 1–5 scoring rubric

- **General scale note:** a score of **5** means the criterion is a visible competitive strength of the project; **3** means the criterion is clearly present but not a standout differentiator; **1** means the evidence shows little support for the criterion or only a minimal/basic showing. Scores **4** and **2** are reserved for intermediate cases.

### Criterion 1 — Innovation and creativity of chosen topic

- **5:** The project tackles a clearly distinctive question, frame, or domain intersection that feels difficult to imitate; the topic choice itself expands what a Fisher-caliber GIS project can be.
- **4:** The topic is strong and thoughtful, with an original angle or unusually sharp framing, but it is not as surprising or paradigm-setting as the strongest cases.
- **3:** The topic is relevant and competently framed, but the novelty comes more from execution than from the problem choice itself.
- **2:** The topic is serviceable but conventional, derivative, or only loosely differentiated from standard classroom GIS work.
- **1:** The evidence does not show a distinctive topic choice or creative framing.

### Criterion 2 — Use of GIS in performing the project

- **5:** GIS is central to the logic of the project, not just its display; the work clearly depends on geospatial methods, platforms, spatial comparison, or remote-sensing / mapping workflows in a sophisticated way.
- **4:** GIS is materially important to the workflow and conclusions, though the method stack is somewhat less ambitious or less integral than the strongest cases.
- **3:** GIS is used competently and appropriately, but mostly at a standard applied level rather than as a major methodological differentiator.
- **2:** GIS appears mainly as a supporting or presentational tool, with limited evidence that it drives the core research logic.
- **1:** The evidence shows only minimal or incidental use of GIS.

### Criterion 3 — Data: complexity, relevance, documentation

- **5:** The project uses unusually rich, multi-source, technically demanding, or especially well-documented data that are tightly matched to the research question.
- **4:** The data are strong, relevant, and reasonably well described, with some complexity or integration beyond routine course-project standards.
- **3:** The data are fit for purpose and adequately described, but they are limited in complexity, integration, or documentation depth.
- **2:** The data appear thin, generic, weakly documented, or only partially aligned with the topic.
- **1:** The evidence provides little confidence in the quality, relevance, or documentation of the data.

### Criterion 4 — Analytical approach and execution

- **5:** The project demonstrates a clear, rigorous analytical design with strong execution, meaningful comparison or modeling logic, and explicit attention to limits or accuracy where relevant.
- **4:** The analysis is solid and persuasive, with real methodological substance, but it is narrower or less rigorous than the strongest cases.
- **3:** The analytical approach is competent and intelligible, but it remains straightforward or only moderately developed.
- **2:** The analysis is present but thin, weakly explained, or not obviously robust.
- **1:** The evidence shows little analytical depth beyond descriptive mapping.

### Criterion 5 — Visualization / cartographic communication effectiveness

- **5:** The cartography or visual narrative substantially improves comprehension, carries a clear argument, and demonstrates strong communication craft appropriate to the topic and medium.
- **4:** The visual communication is strong and effective, with clear design choices and readable storytelling, but not as exceptional or memorable as the best cases.
- **3:** The visualization communicates the project adequately and supports interpretation, but it is not a major competitive differentiator.
- **2:** The visual communication works only partially; design, narrative flow, or cartographic clarity are limited.
- **1:** The evidence suggests weak or ineffective cartographic communication.
