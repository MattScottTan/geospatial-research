\large Where cloud compute is close, where it is far, and why that matters for cities}
\author{EIP submission draft}
\date{March 11, 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
AI is often discussed as a story about companies, labs, and talent. It is also a story about physical infrastructure. Training and deploying modern AI systems depends on cloud regions - the geographic clusters of datacenters run by large providers such as Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud - as well as power and network capacity. Those assets are not spread evenly across space. This report therefore asks a simple question with clear public-interest stakes: do cities that sit farther from major cloud regions also show less observed AI research activity in the delivered overlay, even after accounting for city size and broad regional clustering?

The short answer is yes. Across a global frame of 8,000 cities, AI-linked cities sit much closer to deployed cloud regions than the broader city system, and the negative distance-to-AI relationship remains in two spatial models. The strongest conclusion is that compute accessibility is a robust spatial correlate of observed AI activity. This atlas does \emph{not} prove that opening a cloud region would create an AI hub. It does show that compute geography belongs in the conversation about why AI opportunity is concentrated in some places and thinner in others.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why this matters and what this atlas asks}
AI does not happen on a blank map. Cities participate in AI from very different starting positions. Some sit close to dense clusters of cloud infrastructure. Others are hundreds or thousands of kilometers farther away from the same services. If compute access is part of the enabling environment for AI, then those gaps matter for who can participate, who scales quickly, and which places may be left out of the current wave of AI development.

That makes this atlas useful for more than description. It is designed as a screening tool for judges, planners, and public-interest readers who want to see where compute access is concentrated, where it is thin, and where those infrastructure gaps overlap with weak observed AI activity. Instead of treating cloud infrastructure as invisible background plumbing, the atlas treats it as a measurable part of the geography of AI.

\subsection{The central question}
The report asks a plain-language question: once we account for city size and for the fact that nearby places often resemble one another, do cities that are farther from major cloud regions tend to show less observed AI research activity? It also asks where the largest mismatches remain between large urban populations and weak compute access.

\subsection{The short answer}
The evidence points in the same direction across maps, comparisons, and models. AI-linked cities are much closer to major cloud infrastructure than the wider city system, and that pattern does not disappear after adjusting for population and spatial clustering. The contribution of this report is therefore not a causal estimate. It is a clear spatial baseline showing that compute accessibility is part of the story, and that some cities appear to face a stacked disadvantage of size, weak compute proximity, and no observed AI works in the delivered filter.

\subsection{What this atlas does - and does not do}
This atlas does four things in sequence:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item maps how close large cities are to the nearest AWS, Azure, or Google Cloud region;
  \item compares AI-linked cities with the broader 8,000-city frame;
  \item checks whether the distance pattern survives basic controls for population and geography; and
  \item flags ``priority cities'' where weak compute proximity and weak observed AI activity appear together in the delivered data.
\end{itemize}

What it does \emph{not} do is estimate the causal effect of opening a cloud region. Cloud infrastructure is not placed randomly, so the most honest claim here is about a durable spatial association, not a treatment effect.

The AI layer is a user-provided OpenAlex-derived overlay of city-level AI-related works \citep{openalex}. \section{How the atlas measures the relationship}
\subsection{Measure 1: distance to the nearest cloud region}
In this project, compute accessibility means the great-circle distance in kilometers from a city to its nearest AWS, Azure, or Google Cloud region. This is a simple proxy. It is not a direct measure of latency, price, GPU availability, or service quality. It is used because it is transparent, comparable across thousands of cities, and easy to interpret on a map.

\subsection{What the main model is checking}
At its core, the model asks a simple question: when two cities are similar in size, does the one farther from cloud infrastructure tend to show less observed AI research activity? The main outcome is \(\log(1 + \text{AI works})\), which reduces the visual and statistical dominance of a few very large counts.

The formal regression is
\[
\log(1 + \text{AI works}_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \,\text{distance}_{i,1000} + \beta_2 \,\log(\text{population}_i) + s_i + \varepsilon_i,
\]
where \(\text{distance}_{i,1000}\) is nearest-region distance in 1,000 km units, \(\log(\text{population}_i)\) is a basic city-size control, and \(s_i\) is an added geography term. The null hypothesis is that distance has no relationship with observed AI works. The directional alternative is that greater distance is associated with less observed AI activity.

This is still an observational comparison, not a treatment-effect design. Cloud regions are not randomly assigned across cities, so the distance coefficient should be read as a partial association rather than as the causal effect of moving a city closer to compute.

\subsection{Why the model stays intentionally simple}
The statistical goal here is modest. The report is not trying to build the best possible forecasting model of AI activity. It is trying to answer an interpretable question about distance, city size, and geography. Keeping the driver set simple makes the central relationship easier to explain and keeps the report focused on what EIP judges need to understand.

\subsection{Additional spatial checks and screening layers}
The atlas adds three descriptive tools on top of the distance comparison:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Global Moran's I}: a whole-map check for whether high or low values cluster more than chance would suggest.
  \item \textbf{Local Gi*}: a neighborhood-level check that identifies local hot spots and cold spots.
  \item \textbf{Priority cities}: a screening layer that flags cities with zero observed AI works in the delivered overlay and distance above the current upper-quartile threshold.
\end{itemize}

These tools do different jobs. Moran's I asks whether the pattern is spatially organized at all. Local Gi* asks where unusually strong or weak local clusters appear. The priority screen asks where weak compute access and weak observed AI activity stack together in cities that may deserve closer policy attention. All three are descriptive diagnostics, not causal tests.

\subsection{Two ways of accounting for geography}
The report estimates the same core relationship in two different spatial ways.

\paragraph{Model 1: driver regression plus Gaussian Process residual field.}
The Gaussian Process (GP) version treats unexplained geography as a smooth background surface across the map \citep{rasmussen2006}. In plain English, it is the broad-regional-gradient model: nearby places are allowed to resemble one another because they sit in a similar regional environment.

\paragraph{Model 2: driver regression plus CAR/GMRF city-neighbor effect.}
The CAR/GMRF version instead links each city to nearby cities in a graph and lets neighbors share a local effect \citep{besag1974,rue2005}. In plain English, it is the local-clustering model: it is more aggressive about absorbing similarity among neighboring cities.

Using both is useful because the comparison is informative. If distance remains negative in both models, the result is less likely to be only a city-size artifact or a purely visual map effect. If the distance term shrinks once stronger local pooling is introduced, that suggests some of the raw compute gradient overlaps with broader regional ecosystems.

\section{Results}
The results section answers four practical questions: where compute access is strongest, whether AI-linked cities sit closer to that infrastructure, which places appear most underserved in the delivered data, and whether the distance pattern survives basic spatial checks.

Start with Figure~\ref{fig:accessmap}. The main thing to notice is not any one city. It is the broad split between the dense compute corridors in North America, Europe, and East Asia and the much larger areas where major cloud infrastructure remains relatively far away.

Figure~\ref{fig:surfdist} tells the same story in a smoother way. It is not a routing model or a city-by-city forecast. It is a simple communication surface that makes the broad geography of access easy to read at a glance.

\begin{figure}[ht]

In other words, close access to large-scale cloud infrastructure is not the norm across the global city system. For an EIP audience, that matters because many large cities still experience cloud compute as remote infrastructure rather than as something locally embedded in their urban economy.

\section{What this means for EIP}
The most defensible conclusion is a middle one. Compute accessibility is a spatially robust correlate of observed AI activity, but this report does not identify a causal treatment effect of opening a cloud region. That distinction matters. The atlas is stronger than a visual correlation and weaker than a causal estimate, which is exactly where a careful EIP submission should be.

The practical value is that the atlas turns a broad concern about digital inequality into a more specific set of questions. It helps readers see where the issue looks like infrastructure scarcity, where it looks like concentration around existing hubs, and where large cities may deserve closer follow-up because they appear both far from compute and weak in the delivered AI overlay.

That makes the atlas useful as a screening and communication tool. It can help judges, planners, and public-interest readers ask better next questions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Which regions seem to face a genuine compute-footprint shortage rather than only a local talent gap?
  \item Which large cities deserve closer qualitative follow-up because weak compute access may be stacking with other disadvantages?
  \item Where does the policy challenge look like missing infrastructure, and where does it look more like a concentration of advantages in a few existing hubs?
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations to keep in mind}
The report is designed to stay readable without hiding its limits.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Distance is not latency, price, or capacity.} Straight-line proximity is a transparent first-order proxy, but real access also depends on routing, service availability, GPU allocation, regulation, and price.
  \item \textbf{The OpenAlex overlay is useful but not fully documented.} The repository preserves the delivered files and topic IDs, but not the exact query end date or recency window. The overlay should therefore be read as a defensible observed layer, not as an exhaustive ground truth.
  \item \textbf{The model sample is limited.} The models are fit on 328 matched overlay rows, not the full 8,000-city universe. That is enough to reveal a pattern, but not enough to justify fine-grained forecasting claims.
  \item \textbf{Spatial models do not create causal identification.} GP and CAR/GMRF terms absorb geographic dependence, but they do not turn an observational atlas into a causal estimate.
  \item \textbf{The smooth surfaces are communication products.} The 1 degree rasters are useful for orientation, but they should not be read as fine-resolution predictive maps.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
The report's short answer is the same one stated up front: yes, compute geography appears to matter. Across the 8,000-city comparison frame, AI-linked cities sit closer to major cloud infrastructure than the broader city system, and the negative distance-to-AI relationship remains after adjusting for population and two different forms of spatial dependence. The strongest claim supported by the evidence is that compute accessibility belongs inside the explanation of why today's AI activity is concentrated where it is.

Just as important, the report sets a clear boundary around that claim. It does not show that opening a cloud region would automatically create an AI hub. Cloud-region placement, research depth, energy systems, capital, and institutional strength all evolve together. The right reading is therefore practical rather than triumphant: this atlas is a useful spatial diagnosis of where compute access is strong, where it is weak, and where that weakness appears to overlap with weak observed AI activity.

If the next stage of work needs a causal answer, it should use time variation around major cloud-region openings or other infrastructure shocks. That is the right path if the question shifts from ``where are the gaps?'' to ``does improved compute access change a city's AI trajectory?''
