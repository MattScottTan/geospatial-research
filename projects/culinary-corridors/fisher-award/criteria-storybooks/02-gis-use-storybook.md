# Use of GIS Storybook

## Criterion definition

This criterion rewards projects where GIS is part of the reasoning engine. The strongest accessible winners do not simply present spatial outputs; they rely on geospatial workflows, remote sensing, classification, satellite detection, or spatial scoring methods to produce the substantive result.[P1][P2][P3][P5][P8]

## Evaluation signals

- GIS is named as a method, platform, or workflow rather than remaining implicit.[P2][P3][P5][P8]
- The project would lose explanatory force if the geospatial layer were removed.[P1][P2][P3][P4][P5]
- Spatial comparison, detection, or classification drives the argument.[P2][P3][P5]
- The artifact exposes enough method language that a judge can see how GIS shaped the finding.[P1][P2][P3][P5][P8]
- Maps are used to analyze, rank, compare, or infer — not merely to decorate a narrative.[P2][P3][P5][P8]

## Winner case studies

### Top-tier accessible cases

- **Shane Rice (5/5 in the current matrix):** GIS is inseparable from Shane’s project because the work is explicitly a geospatial analysis of settlement destruction, and the recoverable public evidence points to historically specific imagery collections that had to be spatially interpreted rather than simply displayed.[S5][P2]
- **Dev Patel (5/5):** Dev’s project uses satellite data, machine learning, and geophysical reasoning to detect inundation at granular spatial scale over time. GIS is not an accessory layer here; it is the measurement system.[P3]
- **Bora Ju (5/5):** Bora’s project is a model case of GIS-centered execution because it explicitly uses Google Earth Engine and ArcGIS Pro in a mixed workflow and applies supervised classification to identify land-use change.[P5]

### Strong 4/5 cases

- **Aanchal Chopra (4/5):** Aanchal’s work earns a strong score because cool-route mapping and heat-adjusted walkability are inherently spatial. The public artifact makes clear that GIS is built into the route/index logic.[S4][P1]
- **Beatrice Youd (4/5):** Beatrice’s project treats conservation impacts and accessibility as spatial questions; GIS is necessary to connect protected areas, forest loss, and access patterns.[S6][P4]
- **Emilio Sempris (4/5):** Emilio’s poster shows ArcGIS Pro at the center of data organization, processing, and analysis, even though the final visual object also depends on Blender and Illustrator.[P8]

## Anti-patterns and omissions

- If GIS remains implicit, judges and later readers cannot easily tell whether the project is geospatially original or simply uses mapping as a presentation layer.[P6][P7]
- Public artifacts that foreground narrative but underexpose workflow details become difficult to score confidently on GIS use, even if the project itself may have been strong. That is part of why `CITIES [re]DEFINED` and `HARLEM, NYC` remain `partial`.[S7][P6][S1][P7]
- A future entrant should not assume that “having maps” is enough. The artifact should make the GIS workflow legible in plain language.[P1][P2][P3][P5]

## Win tactics

- Name the platform or workflow directly in the artifact when it matters (for example ArcGIS Pro, Google Earth Engine, classification workflow, satellite detection pipeline).[P2][P3][P5][P8]
- Explain what GIS made possible that a non-spatial workflow would not have revealed.
- Tie each major map or spatial output to a concrete inferential step, not just a visual moment.
- Avoid burying the GIS logic behind domain-specific prose. The spatial method should be visible even to a non-specialist judge.
- If the project is design- or communication-heavy, preserve at least one clear statement showing how GIS shaped the decision logic.[P1][P8]

## Future-submission checklist

- Can a judge point to the exact spatial workflow that drives the result?
- Would the project still “work” without GIS, or does GIS change the answer?
- Are the core platforms, methods, or spatial operations named explicitly?
- Does each map or spatial output perform analytical work?
- Is the GIS contribution legible to a reader outside the project’s domain?
