# Run 3 Final Scope and Claims

Created: 2026-04-29

## Final title

**Culinary Corridors: Mapping Food Similarity, Spatial Residuals, and Regional Exchange**

## Central research question

When two cuisines are similar, is that similarity mostly explained by geographic distance, or do residual similarities reveal spatial corridors, regional bridges, and boundary patterns that distance alone cannot explain?

## Final scope

The project uses a **global discovery screen** followed by **focused geospatial interpretation**:

1. **Global discovery screen:** all 20 cuisines in the recipe corpus are used to identify broad similarity patterns, a distance baseline, and candidate residual culinary corridors.
2. **Primary inference case:** East/Southeast Asia, including Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, and Filipino cuisines where available.
3. **Secondary/diagnostic case:** Iberian/Atlantic-Pacific corridor, including Spanish, Brazilian, Mexican, Filipino, Cajun/Creole, Jamaican, and Southern U.S. where available.
4. **Geospatial-only insight:** residual bridge scores and boundary/permeability summaries translate pairwise food-similarity residuals into mapped spatial roles.

## Thesis paragraph

This project treats cuisine as spatial evidence. It represents cuisines as ingredient profiles, measures pairwise culinary similarity, compares that similarity to geographic distance, and maps the residuals: the pairs and places whose food similarity is stronger than a distance-only model predicts. The global model is a discovery screen rather than a causal explanation. The strongest evidence comes from focused cases, especially East/Southeast Asia, where residual similarities remain spatially organized after generic-ingredient filtering. Residual bridge scores then show that cuisine similarity is not only an ingredient-space phenomenon; it has a mapped spatial structure of corridors, bridges, and boundaries.

## Strong conclusions allowed

- Cuisine similarity is spatially structured in the prototype dataset.
- Straight-line geographic distance explains some, but not all, cuisine similarity.
- Filtering generic ingredients changes the global residual rankings, so global results should be interpreted as discovery rather than proof.
- East/Southeast Asia is the strongest focused case because the available cuisine labels form a coherent regional/cross-subregional subset.
- Residual bridge scores provide the strongest Fisher-style geospatial insight because they convert pairwise residual similarities into mapped place-level roles.
- The project demonstrates a GIS workflow in which spatial modeling produces the insight rather than simply illustrating a preexisting food story.

## Cautious hypotheses allowed

- Some positive residual corridors may be consistent with migration, trade, colonial, maritime, or agricultural exchange histories.
- East/Southeast Asian residual similarities may reflect regional adjacency, shared ingredient ecologies, food technologies, and land/sea exchange zones.
- Iberian/Atlantic-Pacific links may be consistent with long-distance historical exchange, but are more vulnerable to platform bias and coarse cuisine labels.
- Flavor chemistry may become a useful secondary layer after ingredient-to-compound matching and food-science review.

## Forbidden claims

- Do not claim the recipe corpus is representative of all world cuisines.
- Do not claim the model proves migration, trade, empire, colonialism, or maritime exchange caused any cuisine similarity.
- Do not treat cuisine labels as exact nation-state units.
- Do not treat the global residual map as a final causal model.
- Do not make fermentation or flavor chemistry central without new validation.
- Do not describe residual corridors as historical facts; describe them as spatial hypotheses generated by the model.

## Final language rule

Use **“suggests,” “is consistent with,” “candidate corridor,” “spatial association,” and “prototype evidence”** for mechanism language. Reserve **“shows”** for directly computed results: similarity values, distance trends, residuals, case membership, and bridge-score rankings.
