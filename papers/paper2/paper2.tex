\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{[Placeholder abstract --- T-009.]}
A permutation test for spatial association builds its null distribution by
reshuffling the observed values across locations. That construction assumes the
neighbour structure was fixed before the data were seen. In practice the
neighbour parameter is routinely chosen by trying several values and keeping the
one that gives the strongest signal. This paper shows that the practice inflates
the false-positive rate by roughly a factor of three, measures the inflation as a
surface over selection rules and candidate grids rather than as a point, and
derives the correction. Numbers, figures and citations in this draft are
placeholders until the evidence tasks land; every one of them will be traceable
through \texttt{papers/CLAIMS.md}.
\end{abstract}

\section{The mechanism\CONTRIBUTION}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\textbf{[Placeholder --- T-009.]} A permutation test builds its null by
reshuffling observed values across locations, so it assumes exchangeability ---
and exchangeability quietly requires that the weights matrix was fixed in
advance. Selecting $k$ from the data conditions the reported statistic on the
event that it was the maximum, and the unconditional null no longer describes it.

\begin{keyinsight}
The reported p-value answers the question ``how unusual is this statistic?'' when
the question actually asked was ``how unusual is the largest of several
statistics?'' The candidate statistics are strongly correlated, so the inflation
is smaller than a naive multiple-comparisons bound but far from negligible.
\end{keyinsight}

\textbf{[Placeholder --- T-003 supplies the numbers, T-009 the prose.]}
